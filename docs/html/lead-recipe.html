Building Sunset Solo from init

A good lead is mostly about what happens after the note starts. This recipe turns a plain saw into a singing solo, and the key decision is making vibrato arrive late, the way a player does.

Setup

Choose Reset Parameters, then loop a simple melody around A4, with a mix of short and long notes.

Each step's preview adds to the one before. Measurements compare it with the previous step.

  1. Start from init

    A single saw, wide open, with six voices. It has the right harmonic series for a lead but no sense of being played.

    Listen for: A bright, buzzing line. Notes are the same from start to end.

  2. Make it one player

    • Voices6→toMono (1)

    Mono means a new note always replaces the old one. A solo line should feel like a single player with a single instrument, and that's hard to fake with polyphony.

    The other choice: Hero Theme uses two voices, so the release can overlap the next note. That helps a wide, slow melody but sounds less like one player.

    Listen for: Almost no change in this phrase: it matters once notes overlap.

  3. Soften the tone

    • VCF Cutoff10.0 (about 21726 Hz at C4)→to6.2 (about 1284 Hz at C4)
    • VCF Key follow0.0→to5.0

    A wide open saw is tiring. Closing the filter to about 1.3 kHz keeps it vocal rather than buzzy, and key follow keeps high notes from going dull as you climb.

    Listen for: Warmer and less aggressive, while it's still clearly a saw.

    • Brightness: 4,792 Hz → 969 Hz

  4. Shape the note

    • VCF Env0.0→to1.2
    • Env T1 (attack)0.0 (about 1 ms)→to0.5 (about 10 ms)
    • Env T3 (decay)0.0 (about 10 ms to -20 dB)→to5.0 (about 678 ms to -20 dB)
    • Env L3 (sustain)10.0→to7.5
    • VCF Res0.0→to1.2

    A small filter envelope gives each note a slight brightening at the start, like breath or bow pressure. Sustain below full lets the note relax after it speaks. A touch of resonance gives the filter a vocal edge.

    The other choice: A large filter envelope would sound more like a pluck or a brass stab, and would draw attention to every note.

    Listen for: Notes now have a slight front edge, then settle.

    • Held level: -1 dB from peak → -3 dB from peak

  5. Let the note end naturally

    • Env T4 (release)0.0 (about 10 ms to -20 dB)→to3.0 (about 105 ms to -20 dB)

    A short release stops the end of each phrase from sounding chopped. In Normal VCA mode, it directly sets how the note fades.

    Listen for: Phrase endings fade rather than cut off.

    • Tail after note-off: 7 ms → 284 ms

  6. Add vibrato

    • DCO LFO0.0→to0.9
    • LFO Rate0.95 Hz→to5.03 Hz
    • LFO Delay0.00 s→to0.00 s

    About 5 Hz is close to a human vibrato, and 0.9 on the slider gives a gentle swing. But with no delay it starts at the same moment as the note, on every note. That's the giveaway of a machine.

    Listen for: A constant wobble, even on short notes. It sounds a bit seasick.

    • Pitch movement on a held note: 0 cents → 16 cents
    • Pitch movement in the first 400 ms: 0 cents → 14 cents

  7. Make the vibrato arrive late

    • LFO Delay0.00 s→to0.40 s

    This is the key decision. With 400 ms of delay, short notes stay straight and only held notes bloom into vibrato. That's how singers and players behave, and it's what makes it feel played.

    Listen for: Short notes are now straight, and long notes start to sing as they're held.

    • Pitch movement in the first 400 ms: 14 cents → 0 cents

  8. Trim the wheel's vibrato

    • MW to DCO LFO1.0→to0.8

    Init already sends the wheel to this LFO at 1.0, which turned out to be wider than this lead needs. The automatic vibrato is gentle, and the wheel adds more at the peak of a phrase. The wheel's scale is strong, so 0.8 is plenty.

    Listen for: In this preview the wheel rises across the second half, and the vibrato gets wider.

    • Vibrato with wheel up: 56 cents → 45 cents

  9. Put it in a space

    • Delay mixOFF→to-0.56
    • Delay Time1/4→to1/8.
    • Delay feedback2.0→to3.0
    • Reverb mixOFF→to-0.64
    • Reverb size8.0→to5.0

    A dotted 8th delay fills the gaps between notes and adds rhythm without adding notes. A small reverb gives it a room. At these settings neither adds much stereo width: the delay's spread is centred at its default, so this is about depth rather than width.

    The other choice: Turn these off if you'd rather use sends in your DAW, which lets several parts share a space.

    Listen for: The melody now has echoes between notes, and it feels like it's in a real place.

    • Tail after note-off: 284 ms → 600 ms

  10. Set the level

    • Volume5.0→to6.2

    Presets in this bank aim for the same loudness, so you can compare them fairly. This is the finished Sunset Solo.

    Listen for: The finished patch.

    • Loudness: -21.6 LUFS → -18.0 LUFS

What to take away

  • Delayed vibrato is what makes a synth lead feel played: short notes stay straight and only long ones bloom.
  • The mod wheel's vibrato scale is much stronger than the DCO LFO slider's: 0.8 on the wheel gives about as much swing as 2.0 on the slider.
  • A dotted 8th echo fills the gaps between phrases, without adding notes.

Where to go next

Starting points for moving from Sunset Solo to another patch in the bank:

  • Hero ThemeTry turning on unison at 4 voices, spread 0.80, with a quiet square an octave up, then raising the reverb.
  • Arcade SquareSwap the saw for the pulse's Square, open the filter to 8.5, and raise LFO Rate to about 10 Hz.
  • Portamento DreamTry a moving pulse in place of the saw, Portamento All at 4.0, and the chorus on.
